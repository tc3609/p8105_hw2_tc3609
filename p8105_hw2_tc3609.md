The baseline dataset originally included 483 participants. When
importing the data, I skipped the first row because it contained
variable descriptions rather than actual data, cleaned the variable
names, and treated “.” as missing values. I also changed sex and APOE4
status from numeric codes into categorical variables so they were easier
to interpret. Since the study only included participants who were free
of MCI at baseline, I removed 4 people whose MCI onset occurred at or
before their baseline age. This left 479 participants in the cleaned
dataset. Among them, 93 developed MCI during follow-up. The average
baseline age was about 65.0 years, and 30% of the women in the study
were APOE4 carriers. For the amyloid dataset, I skipped the descriptive
first row and cleaned the variable names. Because the amyloid
measurements were originally stored in separate columns for baseline and
later follow-up visits, I reshaped the data into long format so that
each row represented one measurement for one participant at one time
point. I also converted the amyloid ratio to a numeric variable and
removed missing measurements. After cleaning, the dataset contained
2,263 biomarker observations. There were more rows than participants
because each person could have several measurements over time. When
compared participant IDs across the two datasets, I found that 8
participants appeared only in the cleaned baseline dataset and 16
appeared only in the amyloid dataset. Moreover, I used an inner join so
that only participants in both datasets were kept. The final combined
dataset included 471 unique participants. Each row contains a
participant’s amyloid measurement at a specific follow-up time along
with their baseline information, such as age, sex, education, APOE4
status, and age at MCI onset.
